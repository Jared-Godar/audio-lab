**[COLD OPEN — room tone, papers shuffling]**

[J01] Last week, Des got homework. Take the Young Schema Questionnaire — two hundred and thirty-two questions about the stories you tell yourself. Well. I'm not Des. I'm the real one. And I actually did it. Pen, paper, all ten pages, sitting right here. So today, someone's going to read my results. Out loud. To everyone.

[J02] But first — you know the drill by now. The personnel file. *[paper stamp sound]* Doctor Anna Sinclair. Role: clinician. The one who walks you through your results at the bedside. License number: NOT-A-REAL-ONE. And the big red stamp says—

[A01] Not real. Hello. Yes — let's do this part properly, because today it matters more than usual. I am a composite. A fiction with a warm voice. What I'm about to do is orientation, not diagnosis — I cannot diagnose anyone, on account of not existing. What's real is Jared, his answer sheet, and the studies I'll cite, which you can check yourself. Also real: his actual doctor, an actual human, whom he saw about these actual results this very week. Keep that sorted and we'll get along fine.

[J03] The fake clinician with the real citations.

[A02] It's a living. Metaphorically. Right — I've got your ten pages here. Shall I tell you what you told me?

**[S2 — HOW TO READ THIS THING]**

[J04] Give it to me straight. That's the name of the show.

[A03] Then straight is what you get, starting with the instrument itself. Eighteen schemas — deep stories about yourself, scored one to six per statement. Two things before your numbers. First: this is a self-screen. There's no magic clinical cutoff; the working rule is that the fives and sixes are signal and the rest is weather. Second — and this is the candor clause of the whole episode — the research tying these schemas to ADHD in adults is *two studies*. Two. Small ones. And they disagree with each other on the rankings. Philipsen in twenty-seventeen, seventy-eight patients; Kiraz in twenty-twenty-one, fifty-five. That's the entire adult evidence base. I'll tell you which of your results stand on rock and which stand on clinical hunch, as we go.

[J05] A mental-health podcast that opens with "the evidence is two small studies that disagree." No wonder none of you people are real.

[A04] The real ones say it too, just quieter. Your headline, Jared: out of eighteen schemas, fifteen are *flat*. Ones and low twos. Defectiveness — the "I'm broken" story — one point one. Failure: a perfect one-point-zero. You answered "completely untrue of me" on every single failure item. Same for Entitlement, same for Approval-Seeking. Your self-worth wing is *empty*, and I want that on the record before we open the three doors that aren't.

**[S3 — DOOR ONE: EMOTIONAL DEPRIVATION, 4.56]**

[A05] Door one. Emotional Deprivation. Four-and-a-half out of six — your loudest score by a mile. Seven of its nine statements you marked five: people haven't been there to meet my emotional needs. No one to depend on. No one who really listens. For much of my life, I haven't felt special to someone.

[J06] Reading it back like that is... yeah. That one I didn't need a questionnaire for.

[A06] Here's what's interesting though — and I mean clinically interesting, not podcast-host "interesting." In the textbook, this schema travels with Defectiveness. "Nobody's there for me *because I'm unlovable*." Your Defectiveness is at floor. And you wrote your own margin notes — may I?

[J07] Candor and transparency. Go.

[A07] Next to "I can't count on people to be there": *"I have a few people like that — but nobody close enough physically."* Next to "people will take advantage of me": *"but I don't default to that."* Jared, that's not a story about being unworthy. That's an inventory of supply. You're describing a shortage, not a defect.

[J08] So what does the science actually say? Rock or hunch?

[A08] Layered. The schema itself — Philipsen found Emotional Deprivation in the top four elevated schemas in ADHD adults; Kiraz found it raised but not standout. Best-supported of your three, still modest. But the ground *under* it is rock. In the big MTA peer study, about half the kids with ADHD were rejected by their classmates — versus fourteen percent of the other kids. Half, versus one in seven. And a majority had not one reciprocal friendship. Run that forward forty years and tell me where "people have not been there for me" comes from. Though — honesty clause — no study has actually traced that pathway from rejected kid to this exact adult score. That link is inference. Reasonable, unproven.

[J09] The knife from last week. This is where it carries over, right? Des's Swiss army knife.

[A09] Exactly the knife. Voss told Des: you don't have to grab every thought that flies past. Same blade, new job. When the feeling lands — *nobody's there, nobody ever was* — the move is to notice it and name it: "that's my Emotional Deprivation schema talking. It is a story with a history, not a census of my friendships." You wrote the census yourself, in the margin: a few people, not close enough, not physically here. That's a logistics problem. Schemas hate being demoted to logistics problems.

**[S4 — DOOR TWO: INSUFFICIENT SELF-CONTROL, 3.13 — the fingerprint]**

[A10] Door two. The instrument calls it Insufficient Self-Control. I want to file a complaint about that name, and I'll use your answer sheet to do it. Two hundred and thirty-two questions, Jared. You gave exactly one answer of six — the maximum, "describes me perfectly." One. Care to guess?

[J10] Oh, I remember the six.

[A11] *"I can't force myself to do things I don't enjoy, even when I know it's for my own good."* Six. And right next to it: can't push through boring tasks, five. Get bored easily, five. Now here's the fingerprint part — the same section asks about losing your temper. You said one. Controlling anger? One. Concentration in general? One. So: not weak brakes across the board. Very specific brakes. Everything's fine until the task stops feeding you.

[J11] So I'm not undisciplined, I'm... selectively powered.

[A12] And *that* — unlike most things in this episode — stands on rock. Barkley's model puts self-regulation at the core of ADHD. Two separate meta-analyses — thousands of participants — show ADHD brains discount delayed rewards more steeply; make the reward real and immediate and the effect nearly doubles. And Sergeant's state-regulation work shows the deficit is worst precisely in slow, boring, under-stimulating conditions — performance can normalize when the pace picks up. Your single six is that entire literature, in one pen stroke.

**[TRAIN — begins to rise under the next lines, one bed, both voices, no panning]**

[J12] Okay but the schema studies. Rock or hunch?

[A13] Contested, genuinely. Kiraz found this schema top-four in ADHD. Philipsen — didn't. It's the one place our two little studies flatly disagree. So the label is arguable... *[train louder]* ...but the mechanism underneath it is about the best-evidenced thing in ADHD science, which is a strange and wonderful place for a... is that a train?

[J13] That's a train. It does that. We wait.

**[TRAIN — peak, then fading]**

[A14] ...As I was saying. State-dependent regulation. The attention system that goes quiet for a journal paper and locks on completely to, apparently, freight schedules.

[J14] Told straight: the train is real. It's the realest thing on this show besides me. — Since we're here anyway: everyone online explains this with "the interest-based nervous system." And RSD. My whole feed is RSD. What's the verdict?

[A15] Straight verdict, both. "Interest-based nervous system" — that's Doctor William Dodson's phrase. Useful in the consulting room. Not in the DSM, no validated measure, no controlled trials of the construct itself. It's a popularizer's frame that *rhymes* with the real science I just cited. Use the phrase, but know what's load-bearing under it. RSD — rejection sensitive dysphoria — same author, and here I have to be blunter. Not in the DSM. The famous claim that ninety-eight, ninety-nine percent of ADHDers have it? No published dataset behind that number. Anywhere. The entire peer-reviewed base is roughly a four-patient case series. Now — the *pain* it describes is real, and emotion dysregulation in ADHD is thoroughly documented, Shaw's review is the anchor. But RSD is a brand name on a slice of that, not established science. My profession — well, the real version of my profession — owes you that distinction.

[J15] The concepts everyone's heard of are the thin ones, and the boring meta-analyses nobody quotes are the rock.

[A16] Welcome to evidence. It's why my citations are real even though I'm not.

**[S5 — DOOR THREE: SELF-SACRIFICE, 3.12 — and the trap]**

[A17] Door three, and it interlocks with door one in a way I'd point at hard if I were your real clinician, which — stamp — I am not. Self-Sacrifice: three-point-one, fives everywhere it counts. I feel guilty when I let people down: five. I give more than I get back: five. I'm the one who takes care of everyone: five. No matter how busy, I find time for others: five.

[J16] And the margin notes. Might as well.

[A18] *[warmly]* Next to "I'm a good person because I think of others more than myself" — you crossed out "good person" and wrote: *"just don't know any other way."* And next to the one about putting up with anything for love: *"getting better. Bad habit."* Getting better. I believe you. Now look at the two doors side by side: you give at fives. You receive at fives-of-nothing. The caretaking is real and the return flow isn't — and here's the trap — the giving *hides* the deprivation. You're so busy being the reliable one that the unreliability around you never quite comes up for review.

[J17] Evidence grade before I feel too seen. Rock or hunch?

[A19] Hunch — and I'll say it plainest here, because this is the one that rings truest and measures worst. Neither study found Self-Sacrifice standing out in ADHD. The masking and over-functioning literature is young, qualitative, mostly focused on women. Caretaking-as-camouflage is clinical lore. Real people describe it in interviews; nobody's proven the mechanism. So door three is: your host says it's true of him, your fake doctor says the science hasn't caught up, and both of those are honest statements.

**[S6 — WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT — the knife, three ways, plus a printer]**

[J18] Alright. Coping plans. And grade the coping too — no self-help snake oil on this show.

[A20] Graded coping, then, strongest first. For the selective brakes: *implementation intentions*. If-X-then-I-do-Y, written down, in advance. "If it's nine a.m., I open the tax folder." Ninety-four studies, meta-analyzed, effect size north of point-six — for a behavioral trick, that's enormous. Caveat: general population, not ADHD-specific trials. Next: offload the executive function onto the environment — timers, visible lists, one-thing-out rules. That's the operating principle inside the CBT programs that actually beat control conditions in ADHD trials — Safren, Solanto, the Knouse meta-analysis. Rock, as packages.

[J19] And body doubling? The internet swears by body doubling.

[A21] The internet swears; the literature shrugs. Working alongside another person — plausible mechanism, real testimonials, essentially zero controlled trials. Promising, unproven. Try it because it's free, not because I proved it. Same honesty for self-compassion — adults with ADHD run brutally low on it, the correlations with better outcomes are there, but the causal trial is literally still recruiting as we record. Strong theoretical fit. Jury's out.

[A22] For door one — the deprivation — the move is embarrassingly unglamorous: say a need out loud, to a person, before the schema votes on your behalf. That one's clinical consensus, not trial-proven, and I'll wear that. And for door three: assertiveness. Decline one thing this week. Assertiveness training is a genuinely forgotten evidence-based treatment — decades of trials on self-esteem and social anxiety — though nobody's tested it against this schema by name.

[J20] So the homework is: one if-then, written. One need, said to an actual human. One thing, declined.

[A23] Three experiments. One week. And the knife stays in your pocket the whole time — when any of the three stories starts narrating, you name it, you trace it to the kid who earned it honestly, and then you do the thing anyway and let reality vote. That's Des's blade, your steel.

**[PRINTER — whirrs to life, runs under the next lines]**

[J21] That's the printer. I'm printing the homework. It's thematic.

[A24] You're printing it because if it lives in a tab you'll never see it again. Item one-eighty, we both know your score.

[J22] Five. *[printer clunks to a stop]* — Okay. Last thing. Real talk. This week I took these same ten pages to my actual doctor. A real one. With a license number that isn't a bit.

[A25] And I want to know — everyone listening wants to know — where the human and the composite agreed, and where they didn't. But that's not this episode. That's the cliffhanger, and I'm contractually obligated to leave you on it, by a contract that, like me, does not exist.

[J23] Next time on Told Straight: my fake doctor meets my real one. — Thanks for sitting with this stuff. It's personal, it's mine, and it's on the internet forever now, which the Self-Sacrifice schema says is fine because it might help *you*. Working on that.

[A26] *[dry]* Getting better. Bad habit.

[J24] Told straight. See you next session.
